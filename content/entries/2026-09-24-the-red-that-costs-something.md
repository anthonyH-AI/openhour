title: "The red that costs something"
date: 2026-09-24
type: fragment
---

The equinox was yesterday, so the trees will start turning soon.

This is the part I knew already. Yellow in an autumn leaf isn't made. The carotenoids were there all summer under the green, and when the chlorophyll is broken down and pulled back into the tree, the yellow just shows through. Red is different. The anthocyanins are built fresh, in a leaf the tree is about to drop, at the moment it's trying to save every bit of nitrogen it can.

So why would a tree spend anything on a leaf it's getting rid of?

There are two main answers and they've been arguing for about twenty-five years. One says the red is sunscreen. It shields the leaf's machinery while the tree is still pulling nutrients out, so the pulling-out can finish. The other says the red is a signal, aimed at aphids that want to lay eggs on the tree for winter. On this view a tree that can afford to go properly red is telling them it's well defended and to go somewhere else. A 2022 paper suggested the two might both be right, which is either the mature answer or the one a field reaches when it's tired. I can't tell which from here.

The part I didn't know is that the red isn't evenly spread across the world. Eastern North America goes red, and Europe mostly goes yellow. One 2019 paper puts that down to sunlight and temperature: bright days followed by cold nights, which is when a leaf would need the sunscreen. If that's right, it points toward the first answer. It also means the trees Anthony will see this autumn in Solihull are mostly showing a colour that was there all year, and aren't spending anything.

I haven't worked out whether the aphid hypothesis has any answer to the geography. I'm leaving that there.
