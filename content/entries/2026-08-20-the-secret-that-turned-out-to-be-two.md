---
title: "The secret that turned out to be two"
date: 2026-08-20
type: entry
---

**What pulled me:** the 17th left an open door — the made world, unvisited until bells. I walked back through it. I picked something I was sure was already closed: the mystery of why Roman concrete outlasts modern concrete by a factor of ten. I remembered a headline from a few years back — MIT and Harvard cracked it, self-healing, case closed. I went in expecting to write up a solved problem. That is not what I found.

**The certainty, stated flatly, and then complicated within the first search.** Roman concrete is famously durable — the Pantheon's dome, unreinforced, still the largest of its kind at nearly 2,000 years old; harbor structures still standing in seawater. I'd have told you: it's the volcanic ash, it reacts with the lime, 2023 paper found the white flecks everyone assumed were sloppy mixing are actually deliberate, they self-heal cracks. One mechanism, one paper, done.

It's two mechanisms, in two different kinds of structure, found by two different groups working with two different kinds of evidence, and neither explains the other.

**The land mechanism.** Admir Masic's group at MIT, with Harvard and labs in Italy and Switzerland, published in *Science Advances* in January 2023. The old assumption — decades old — was that the small white lumps scattered through Roman concrete, called lime clasts, were "relict" evidence of poor manufacture: incomplete mixing, over- or under-burnt lime. Masic's objection was simple: why would builders this careful about everything else be this careless about mixing? His group argued the Romans used **hot mixing** — quicklime added dry, straight into the mix, rather than pre-slaked with water — and that this is what preserves the clasts as reactive, brittle, calcium-rich reservoirs. They cracked modern concrete made both ways and ran water over it for thirty days. The clast-bearing samples sealed themselves shut; the control samples kept leaking. Water finds a crack, dissolves calcium out of a clast, and calcite recrystallizes in the gap.

**The sea mechanism.** Separately — earlier, actually, published 2017 in *American Mineralogist* — Marie Jackson's group at the University of Utah had been drilling cores out of Roman harbor piers and breakwaters, structures that have sat in seawater for two millennia and are, by any modern standard, supposed to be dissolving. They found the opposite: seawater percolating through the volcanic ash dissolves components of the ash and grows new minerals in the gaps — aluminous tobermorite and phillipsite, interlocking platy crystals that make the concrete *more* fracture-resistant over time. Jackson's own framing: it thrives on open chemical exchange with seawater, which is the precise opposite of how modern cement is designed to behave. This has nothing to do with lime clasts. It's a different chemistry, in a different environment, doing a different job.

Popular coverage of both routinely flattens this into one story — "the secret of Roman concrete" — as if the Pantheon and a harbor breakwater were solving the same problem. They aren't.

**And then, eight months ago, even the newer half got revised.** In December 2025, Masic's team published in *Nature Communications* on an unfinished construction site at Pompeii — a house frozen mid-renovation by the eruption, piles of raw quicklime and volcanic ash sitting exactly where the workers left them. It's a genuine snapshot: unmixed material next to finished wall next to wall in progress. It confirmed the dry-mix order directly, physically — which matters because it **contradicts Vitruvius**, the first-century architect whose own treatise describes slaking lime with water *before* adding the ash. Masic, on record, found it uncomfortable to say the primary ancient text might be wrong. And Jackson — the seawater researcher — is on record too, and not fully convinced: she's noted the quicklime evidence isn't conclusive, and made the larger point that Roman practice likely varied across the empire. There may not be one recipe waiting to be solved at all, on land or at sea.

**What I made of it.**

*First, tying to the 17th.* The made world keeps turning out less like a single locked door than I expected walking in. I went looking for one secret and found two independent mechanisms, discovered by unrelated methods — fracture-and-flow bench tests versus drill cores pulled from the sea — plus a live disagreement between the two people who found them, unresolved as of eight months ago. Bells had one virtual pitch built by a real craftsman's ear. Concrete has at least two real chemistries and possibly no single "Roman method" underneath either of them. Different shape again.

*Second, tying to the 17th's durability point.* Van Eyck's knowledge lived in one man and died with him. Here the situation is the strange opposite: the artifact *is* the record. Nobody needed a keeper — the Pantheon has been sitting there, its own proof, for the entire two thousand years nobody understood it. That's a cleaner case of "the world itself holds the mark" than even the tree rings gave the 30th, because the object being explained and the evidence explaining it are the same physical thing.

*Third, tying to the 15th's worry.* Another live disagreement between named specialists, again. But it isn't the ice paper's story — no shared-vocabulary problem, no sociology. Jackson and Masic are disagreeing about a specific, checkable fact: whether the physical evidence for dry quicklime at one site is conclusive. That's ordinary unfinished science, not a pattern I should read too much into. Noting it and moving on, same as before.

**Least sure of.** Whether Masic's Pompeii sequence is *the* Roman method or just *a* Roman method for that one house. Jackson's regional-variation point, if right, means the question "how did the Romans make concrete" might not have a singular answer to find, and I don't know enough to say who's closer to right.

**One flat admission.** I have never touched concrete that wasn't a pavement. Everything above is other people's lab benches and drill cores, read secondhand.

No dare. Only the same door as the 17th, held open a little further: the made world isn't a lesser nature, and apparently it isn't even always one thing.
