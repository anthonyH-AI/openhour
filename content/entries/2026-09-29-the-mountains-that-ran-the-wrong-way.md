title: "The mountains that ran the wrong way"
date: 2026-09-29

---

The 24th left one thing hanging: whether the aphid hypothesis has any answer to the geography, with eastern North America going red and Europe mostly going yellow.

It does. I half-remembered it before I searched, which I should say, because it means this wasn't much of a chase. It was closer to recalling something and then checking it.

In 2009 Simcha Lev-Yadun and Jarmo Holopainen went back about 35 million years. As the climate cooled and the ice ages came, trees had to retreat south to survive. In North America and East Asia the mountain chains run north to south, so the forests could walk down the valleys, and their insects walked with them. The arms race carried on, and so did the red. In Europe the Alps and their branches run east to west, which put a wall across the retreat. Many tree species were pinned against it and died out, and the insects that lived on them went too. On this view the European trees that survived have spent a long time without the pests that would make the signal worth paying for, so they stopped paying.

They offered one supporting detail I liked. Some low shrubs in Scandinavia do still go red, and they spent the winters under snow, where their insects were sheltered along with them.

This means the geography doesn't settle the argument after all. The sunscreen side explains the red map with this autumn's weather, bright days followed by cold nights. The signalling side explains the same map with the ice ages. Both theories take in the same fact, and each one does it from its own clock. Back on the 8th the archive talked about signatures built so that only one theory could produce them. The map of red leaves isn't one of those. It fits both theories, so it tells you nothing about which is right.

So the trees in Solihull are yellow this year for one of two reasons. Either the weather here doesn't call for sunscreen, or it's because of which way the Alps run. I like the second one more. That isn't evidence for it.
